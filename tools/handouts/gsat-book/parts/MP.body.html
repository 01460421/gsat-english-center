The Taipei Botanical Garden, in the heart of the city, is home to more than two thousand kinds of plants. On a guided walk, visitors stop at six signs along the paths. Each sign tells a short story about a remarkable plant.

ALotus

Every summer, the pond here fills with pink and white lotus flowers. Rain never stays on their leaves for long. Tiny bumps and a coat of wax make each water drop roll off, and the rolling drops carry dirt away. The seeds and roots of the plant are also popular foods in Chinese cooking.

BBamboo

It looks like a tree, but bamboo is really a kind of grass. Some kinds are among the fastest-growing plants on Earth. A new shoot can reach its full height in a single season, although it takes a few more years to become strong.

CBodhi Tree

Buddhists believe that the Buddha found the answers he was looking for while sitting under a tree like this one. Its heart-shaped leaves end in a long, thin tip. The tip lets rain run off quickly, so each leaf dries fast.

DFerns

Ferns have no flowers and no seeds. Turn a leaf over, and you may see rows of small brown dots. These dots hold spores, tiny cells that the wind carries away to start new plants. Ferns grow best in cool, shady places.

ECactus

A cactus can survive long dry periods. Its thick stem stores water, and its sharp spines are really leaves that have changed shape. Very little water escapes through these thin spines, and they also protect the plant from hungry animals.

FIndian Almond Tree

This tree adds color to the garden. Before its large leaves fall, they turn bright red. New leaves grow back quickly, so the tree does not stay bare for long. Its wide branches also give visitors plenty of shade.

MP-03進階得分

Every word you read on a page or a screen appears in a particular style of letters, known as a typeface. Most readers never give it a second thought, but each style was shaped by a person, a time, and a purpose. Here are the stories behind six well-known typefaces.

AGaramond

In Paris in the 1500s, Claude Garamond cut metal letters that made books pleasant to read for hours. After his death, his tools were sold and copied across Europe, and versions of his letters still fill the pages of many novels today.

BFutura

German designer Paul Renner released Futura in 1927. He built its letters from simple shapes such as circles, triangles, and squares, giving them a clean, modern look. In 1969, it was chosen for the plaque that the Apollo 11 astronauts left on the Moon.

CTimes New Roman

In 1932, The Times newspaper in London began printing in a new typeface from Stanley Morison. The paper wanted letters that were easy to read yet narrow enough to fit more words on each line. Times New Roman later became a standard choice for school reports.

DCourier

IBM asked Howard Kettler to design Courier for its typewriters in 1955. Every letter takes up exactly the same amount of space, so a thin “i” is as wide as a fat “m.” Because IBM did not keep the design to itself, it soon appeared on typewriters everywhere.

EHelvetica

Created in Switzerland in 1957, Helvetica was meant to look calm and neutral rather than stylish. Its name comes from the Latin word for Swiss, and it is still among the most popular typefaces in the world. It has appeared on New York subway signs, countless company logos, and even the side of America’s space shuttles, though some designers find it boring.

FComic Sans

In 1994, Vincent Connare at Microsoft saw cartoon characters in a program speaking in a stiff, formal typeface, so he drew a playful one based on comic books. Comic Sans has been laughed at ever since, yet scientists at a physics laboratory in Europe once used it to announce a major discovery.

Along many coasts, underwater meadows cover the sandy floor of shallow, sunny bays. Few visitors notice them, yet they matter to wildlife and people alike. Below, a marine biologist explains what these plants are and why they are worth saving, and a fisher describes the changes he has seen in his own bay.

AGardens Beneath the Waves

Swimmers who brush against seagrass often assume it is seaweed, but the two are not even close relatives. Seaweeds are algae, while seagrasses are flowering plants with true roots, leaves, flowers, and seeds, much like the grass in a park. Because they need sunlight, they live only in shallow, clear water. Their thick leaves slow down waves and trap loose mud, which keeps the water clear and protects the shore. Small fish hide among the leaves until they are big enough to survive in open water, while sea turtles and dugongs come to eat. The meadows also lock away carbon in the mud beneath them: although they cover only about 0.1 percent of the ocean floor, they store up to 18 percent of the ocean’s carbon. Sadly, an estimated 7 percent of them disappear every year, mainly because of coastal building, dirty water, and warming seas.

BInterview: A Fisher Remembers

Fisher Tomas Ruiz has worked the same bay for over forty years. “When I was a boy, the grass was so thick that my father’s boat seemed to sail across a green field,” he says. “We always caught plenty of fish near it, because the young ones spend their early lives there.” Over time, more and more visitors arrived in pleasure boats and dropped their anchors straight into the meadow. “Every anchor tore out a patch of plants, and the bare spots kept spreading. Then the water turned cloudy, and our catches got smaller.” For a while, Ruiz feared the grass was gone for good. Then the town put in floating rings that boats could tie up to instead of anchoring. “Some of the empty patches near the harbor have started to recover,” he says. “It is slow, but I can see it.”

MP-05進階得分

Today it is hard to picture an airport without rolling luggage. Yet carts and wagons have rolled along roads for thousands of years, while bags on wheels are a fairly recent idea. Below, a short history of the invention is followed by a travel writer’s column on why it arrived so late.

AHow Luggage Learned to Roll

People tried putting wheels on luggage long before the idea caught on. Patents were issued for a wheeled trunk in 1887 and for a wheeled suitcase in 1945, but neither attracted many buyers. The real turning point came in 1970. Bernard Sadow, an executive at a luggage company, was struggling with two heavy suitcases at an airport in Puerto Rico when he watched a worker push a heavy machine along on a small platform with wheels. Back home, he fixed four small wheels under a suitcase and attached a strap so that it could be towed like a dog on a leash. Department stores in New York turned him down, insisting that nobody would ever pull a bag behind them, until Macy’s agreed to give it a try. Still, his design was far from perfect. In 1987, an airline pilot named Robert Plath stood the bag on its end, put two wheels at the bottom, and added a long handle that slides back inside. Pilots and flight attendants were quick to adopt it, and ordinary travelers soon followed.

BTravel Column: The Bag That Came Late

Whenever I watch travelers glide through an airport, I wonder why it took so long for anyone to put wheels on a suitcase. The technology was hardly the problem. My guess is that, for most of history, people seldom had to carry their own bags very far. Porters waited at every train station and hotel, happy to take your luggage for a small tip. Things changed when the number of people flying grew rapidly and airports became huge, leaving passengers to haul their belongings down endless corridors by themselves. Pride may have played a part as well. My grandfather, who traveled constantly for work, thought rolling bags looked silly and carried his heavy case by hand until his back finally made him give in. Perhaps we simply stop noticing a problem once it becomes part of everyday life. Every time I roll my bag onto a train, I am grateful that somebody finally thought of it.

MP-06頂標得分

In most countries, a family name is passed down from parents to children, but Iceland does things differently. Below, a language writer explains how Icelandic last names work, and an Icelander living in Canada describes what happened when her family’s names were seen through foreign eyes.

ALast Names, Icelandic Style

Most Icelanders do not have a family name at all. Instead, a person’s last name is built from the first name of a parent, usually the father, with -son added for a son and -dóttir for a daughter. If a father named Jón has a son called Gunnar and a daughter called Katla, for example, they will be Gunnar Jónsson and Katla Jónsdóttir. So a brother and a sister do not share a last name. Some last names come from the mother’s first name instead. A last name therefore tells you who someone’s parent is, not which family they belong to, and it stays the same after marriage. That is also why the Icelandic phone book is arranged by first name rather than by last name. First names, on the other hand, are closely guarded. Parents who choose a new one must wait until a naming committee has approved it, and names that cannot take the endings of Icelandic grammar are usually turned down.

BNames on the Move

When my family moved to Canada, I was ready for the cold but not for the attention our names would get. I am Sigrún Ólafsdóttir, which simply means that my father is called Ólafur. My husband is Páll Einarsson, our son is Ari Pálsson, and our daughter is Helga Sigrúnardóttir. We gave Helga my name because I wanted one of our children to carry it. On our first family trip to the United States, a border officer studied our passports for a long time. Then, out of the blue, he asked whether the children were really mine. He clearly doubted that I was their mother, and we nearly missed our flight. Back in Iceland, nobody would have given our names a second look. Still, I would never trade my name for a family name, because it keeps my father’s name with me wherever I go.

MP-07頂標得分

Few of us like feeling bored, and with a phone always within reach, we rarely have to. But what if boredom actually has a job to do? Below, a science writer explains what researchers have learned about this feeling, and a high school student describes two weeks he spent in a village without Wi-Fi.

AWhat Boredom Is Telling Us

People often describe boredom as simply having nothing to do, but psychologists define it more precisely: it is the frustrating feeling of wanting to do something meaningful and being unable to find anything that works. Seen this way, boredom is not a sign of laziness. Instead, it acts like an alarm, warning us that our present activity no longer meets our needs and that it is time to switch to something different. Boredom may also be useful in a less obvious way. When nothing holds our attention, the mind starts to wander, and in these idle moments people often combine ideas in unexpected ways. In one well-known experiment, people who first spent time on a very dull task gave more creative answers afterward than people who had skipped it. The trouble is that we rarely give boredom a chance to do its work. The moment we feel restless, we reach for the nearest distraction, usually a phone, and researchers argue that this habit is a poor way to cope, since it may leave us even more bored than before.

BTwo Weeks Without Wi-Fi

Last summer, I spent two weeks with my grandparents in a mountain village where there was no Wi-Fi and the phone signal came and went. The first two days were miserable. I played the same offline game on my phone over and over, but every time I finally put it down, I felt emptier than before. On the third afternoon, I was so fed up with doing nothing that I asked my grandfather to show me how to repair the rusty bicycle in his shed. Working side by side, we talked more in one afternoon than we had in years. The repairs turned out to be strangely satisfying, and as my hands worked, my mind kept drifting until an idea for a comic about a bicycle that dreams of flying suddenly came to me. By the time I left, half a notebook was full of drawings. I couldn’t wait to get my Wi-Fi back, of course, but now I leave my phone in another room for an hour every evening. Looking back, those two dull weeks were a blessing in disguise.

MP-08頂標得分

Ships no longer have to sail all the way around South America to get from the Atlantic to the Pacific. Text A tells how engineers carried them over the mountains of Panama instead, and in Text B, a ship’s officer describes what crossing the canal is like today.

AOver the Hills by Water

In the 1880s, a French company set out to dig a canal across Panama that would run at sea level from one ocean to the other. Cutting through the mountains proved far harder than its engineers had expected, and tropical diseases such as yellow fever and malaria killed about 20,000 people. Before the decade was over, the project had failed. When the United States took over in 1904, its engineers decided that instead of cutting the hills down to the sea, they would lift ships up over them. They built a dam across the Chagres River, and the valley behind it slowly turned into Gatun Lake, about 26 metres above the sea. At each end of the canal, three locks work like a flight of stairs, raising ships to the lake and lowering them again on the far side. The locks use no pumps. When a valve is opened, water simply runs downhill from the lake into a lock and lifts the ship inside it. Rain falling on the forests around the lake keeps it full. The locks that opened with the canal in 1914 are still in use today.

BA View from the Bridge

I have crossed the canal more than a hundred times, and I still go out on deck to watch every lock. The old locks were built for the ships of 1914. Today’s cargo ships are much wider, so with very little room on either side, every trip through them is a tight squeeze. Small electric engines called mules run on tracks beside each lock and hold us in the middle with steel cables. Then the gates close behind us, water from the lake pours in from below, and the whole ship rises without a sound. Each crossing sends about 200 million litres of that fresh water out to sea. Everything here relies on the rain. In 2023, a long drought left the lake so low that the canal cut the number of ships it let through each day, and the line of ships waiting to cross grew to about 160. The newer locks, which opened in 2016, save part of their water in side pools. Yet no lock can make it rain.
