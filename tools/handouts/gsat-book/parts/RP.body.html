In Japan, a gift sometimes arrives wrapped not in paper but in a square piece of cloth. This cloth, called a furoshiki, is <b>a jack of all trades</b>: it can hold a lunch box, a bottle of water, a pile of books, or a present for a friend. When the job is done, the cloth is simply opened, folded, and kept for next time.

The habit of wrapping things in cloth has deep roots in Japan. It is said to go back to the Nara period, more than a thousand years ago, when valuable objects were wrapped in cloth to keep them safe. The name furoshiki, however, comes from the Edo period, which lasted from 1603 to 1867. In Japanese, "furo" means bath, while "shiki" means to spread. At that time, many people went to large public baths. Before bathing, they wrapped their clothes and other things in a cloth so that their belongings would not get mixed up with those of other people.

The cloth was useful outside the bath, too. People going on long journeys packed what they needed into big cloth bundles and carried them on their backs. Many old Japanese prints show travelers walking along roads with such bundles. Over time, all wrapping cloths of this kind came to be known by the same name.

The cloth found a new role in 2006, when Yuriko Koike, then Japan's Minister of the Environment, designed a special one made from recycled plastic bottles. It was decorated with birds and flowers painted by Ito Jakuchu, an artist of the Edo period. She hoped people would carry their shopping in cloth rather than in plastic or paper bags. The Japanese have a word for the feeling she wanted to share: "mottainai," which means something like "What a waste!"

Visitors to Taiwan are often surprised when they hear Beethoven's "Für Elise" playing in the street in the evening. The music does not come from an ice cream truck or a concert. It comes from a garbage truck, and it tells everyone nearby that it is time to take out the trash.

Musical garbage trucks have been part of daily life in Taiwan since the 1960s. Besides "Für Elise," many trucks play another piano piece, "A Maiden's Prayer." In most places, the trucks come five days a week and usually arrive late in the afternoon or in the evening. When people hear the tune, they hurry out of their homes with bags of trash in their hands.

This system was not always in place. Years ago, during a time of fast economic growth, Taiwan produced far more garbage than it could handle, and landfills were overflowing. To solve the problem, the government decided that trash should no longer be left on the ground for workers to pick up. Instead, people must sort their own trash and bring it to the trucks themselves. General waste goes into a yellow truck, while recycling and food waste are put into bins on a separate vehicle. In Taipei, people must also buy special blue bags for their general waste. As a result, the city now recycles nearly 67 percent of its trash, up from just 2 percent in 2000.

For some people, the fixed schedule is <b>a mixed blessing</b>: it is easy to remember, but it is a problem for those who are busy or away from home. For many older people, however, taking out the trash is now a social activity. They arrive early to chat with their neighbors, and some say the short walk is good exercise. Every evening, the familiar music brings a whole neighborhood together for a few minutes.

RP-03基礎得分

Small cuts are part of everyday life, and most of us take care of them in seconds. We simply open an adhesive bandage, peel off the paper, and press it on. About a hundred years ago, however, caring for a small wound was not so easy. The bandages of that time were large and clumsy, and it was hard to put one on without help. The handy strip we use today came from a husband who wanted to make life easier for his wife.

The husband was Earle Dickson, who bought cotton for the company Johnson & Johnson. His wife, Josephine, often cut or burned her fingers while cooking. Dickson's company already made surgical tape and gauze, but it sold them as two separate products, so he decided to put them together. He stuck small pieces of clean gauze onto a long strip of tape and covered the sticky side with a stiff cloth so that the tape would not stick to itself. Now Josephine could cut off a piece and put it on by herself.

When Dickson showed his idea to the company's leaders, what caught their attention was that a person could put on the bandage without anyone's help. The company began selling the product, called the Band-Aid, in 1920. However, the Band-Aid <b>got off to a slow start</b>: the bandages were made by hand, and only about $3,000 worth were sold in the first year. Things improved after the company handed out large numbers of free Band-Aids to groups of Boy Scouts. In 1924, machines began making them in different sizes, and in 1939 they became fully sterilized.

Dickson stayed with the company and later became one of its vice presidents. The basic design of his bandage has changed very little, and more than 100 billion of them have been made. In 2017, long after his death, he was added to the National Inventors Hall of Fame. His story shows that a great invention does not always begin in a laboratory. Sometimes it begins in a kitchen, with someone who simply wants to help a person he loves.

Xiaoliuqiu is a small coral island off the southwest coast of Taiwan, about 30 minutes by boat from Donggang. Many visitors come here with one special hope: to see a green sea turtle, or even to swim next to one.

Green sea turtles get their name from the color of their fat, not their shells. Adult turtles eat mostly seaweed and sea grass, and many of them feed in the shallow water around the island. Because fishing nets have been banned near the island, the number of turtles living there has grown in recent years. In 2023, the Ocean Conservation Administration counted an average of 637 turtles a month in the waters around Xiaoliuqiu. Between May and October, female turtles also <b>come ashore</b>, crawling up onto the island's beaches to lay their eggs in the sand.

Divers and snorkelers now play an important part in protecting the turtles. Since 2017, a group called TurtleSpot Taiwan has asked people to send in photos of the turtles they meet. The key is the turtle's face. The size, shape, and arrangement of the scales around each turtle's eyes are different, much like human fingerprints. By comparing photos, researchers can tell one turtle from another and learn where each one lives. The photos also help them check whether a hurt turtle has become healthy again after being released, and how many turtles have been injured by fishing lines.

Visitors, however, must follow some rules. Sea turtles are protected by law in Taiwan, so people must not touch, chase, or feed them. Anyone who disturbs a turtle can be sent to prison for up to one year or fined up to NT$300,000. Local groups also clean up trash on the beaches so that mother turtles have a safe place to lay their eggs. With this help from many people, Xiaoliuqiu has become one of the best places in Taiwan to see these gentle animals.

RP-05基礎得分

When someone in Taiwan calls 119 for help, an ambulance usually arrives within minutes. In 2017, the average wait was just under six minutes. Inside, trained workers can start caring for the patient right away. In this way, a modern ambulance is like <b>a hospital on wheels</b>: patients begin receiving treatment on the road, long before they reach the hospital itself. Yet the first ambulances looked very different.

They began on the battlefield. Dominique Jean Larrey was a French army doctor who became the chief doctor in Napoleon's army. In his day, wounded soldiers were usually left where they fell until the fighting was over, so many waited hours or even days for help. In 1797, Larrey built special carts pulled by horses to carry the wounded away during the battle. People called them flying ambulances. His teams also cared for soldiers from both sides.

Later, the idea moved from the army to the city. After the American Civil War, cities such as Cincinnati, New York, London, and Paris started their own ambulance services. In 1869, Edward Dalton, a former army doctor, set up a program in New York City. His ambulances carried bandages, splints, and a box of medicines. By around 1900, young doctors often rode along, but the drivers themselves had almost no medical training. Before long, carriages powered by electricity, steam, or gasoline began to take the place of horses.

Progress did not always continue, however. After World War II, many American ambulances were really hearses, and about half were run by funeral home workers, most of whom had never taken a first-aid class. A report in 1966 showed how poor emergency care was, and the government began paying to train emergency medical technicians, or EMTs. Today, Taiwan also trains EMTs at three levels, and its fire departments send out ambulances more than a million times a year.

Before the French Revolution, a trader moving from town to town could find that familiar units had quietly changed. The country used hundreds of different measures, and even units with the same name did not agree. A pinte of wine, for example, held 0.93 liters in Paris but 1.46 liters in Saint-Denis, a town just to the north. Such confusion made cheating easy and slowed down trade.

After the revolution began, French scientists were asked to create one system for the whole nation. Rather than simply adopt the measures of Paris, which they considered arbitrary, they turned to nature. In 1791 they proposed that a new unit, the metre, should equal one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. Because such a length belonged to the planet rather than to any town or king, they hoped other countries would accept it as well.

Nobody could walk from the pole to the equator, of course, so the plan was to measure one slice of that line and work out the rest. In 1792 two astronomers, Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain, set out to survey the stretch from Dunkirk on the northern coast through Paris to Barcelona in Spain. They measured angles between survey stations, some on mountain tops, and turned the angles into distances. The work was slow and risky: France was in the middle of a revolution, and in 1793 a war with Spain stopped Méchain’s work in Barcelona.

Méchain also carried a secret. He had noticed that two sets of his readings for Barcelona did not match, and, afraid of being blamed, he told no one and later changed some figures. Delambre discovered <b>this</b> only after Méchain’s death, but by then the metre was already law, so he kept quiet. The fieldwork ended in 1798, and the next year a platinum bar was made as the official metre. It was about 0.2 millimeters shorter than planned.

Even then, many French people held on to their old units, and the metric system was not firmly restored in France until 1837. Since 1983, the metre has been defined not by the Earth but by light: it is the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second.

When Wangari Maathai learned in 2004 that she had won the Nobel Peace Prize, she did not throw a party. Instead, she went outside and planted a tree. For a woman whose life's work had begun with a handful of seedlings, it was the most natural way to celebrate.

Maathai was born in 1940 to a farming family in the highlands of central Kenya. Unusually for a girl of her generation, she went on to study biology in the United States. After returning home, she earned a doctorate, something no woman in East or Central Africa had done before, and she later taught at the University of Nairobi. Yet her attention was increasingly drawn to the hardships of rural women, who lacked clean water and wood for their cooking fires.

In 1977, Maathai started the Green Belt Movement, which taught women to plant trees on their farms and around schools and churches. The trees helped keep water supplies clean and provided fuel, while the women who tended them gained skills and a sustainable way to support their families. Over the years, the movement has helped plant over 40 million trees in Kenya.

Her work did not win universal approval. Those in power at the time regarded her as a threat, yet she persevered, and planting trees became a peaceful way of defending ordinary people and their rights. <b>This</b> eventually earned her respect far beyond Kenya. In 2002, she was elected to the national parliament with 98 percent of the vote, and two years later she was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize, the first African woman ever to win it.

Why would a peace prize go to someone known for planting trees? Maathai explained that many conflicts begin as struggles over limited resources such as land and water. When those resources are damaged or scarce, competition for them grows, and competition can lead to violence. Managing nature responsibly, she argued, is therefore one way of protecting peace. She died in 2011, but her legacy endures: the African Union now honors her every year on March 3, known as Wangari Maathai Day.

Checking tomorrow's weather takes most of us only a few seconds. It is easy to forget that the very idea of predicting the weather was once mocked, and that the man who first offered forecasts to the public paid a heavy price for his ambition.

That man was Robert FitzRoy, a naval officer who had once commanded the famous ship HMS Beagle. In 1854, he was put in charge of a small new government office whose task was to study the weather at sea. Five years later, a fierce storm struck the coasts of Britain and wrecked more than a hundred ships. Among them was the Royal Charter, which was on the final stage of a two-month voyage from Australia when it was driven onto rocks in Wales. About 460 people on board died.

FitzRoy was convinced that the disaster could have been predicted. He arranged for stations along the coast to send measurements of air pressure, wind and temperature to London by telegraph every morning. By comparing these reports, his office could detect a storm before it arrived. In February 1861, it issued its first storm warning, and cones and drums were raised on poles in harbors to alert sailors. That August, FitzRoy went a step further: he printed a forecast for the general public in The Times, the first of its kind.

Not everyone welcomed <b>the move</b>. His forecasts were often wrong, and many scientists remained skeptical that the weather could ever be predicted. Although he persisted in refining his methods, the criticism wore him down. After his death in 1865, a government report recommended that forecasting be stopped, and both the forecasts and the storm warnings came to an end. Public demand brought the warnings back in 1867, and forecasts for the public finally returned in 1879.

FitzRoy's service for sailors survives today as the Shipping Forecast, which is believed to be the oldest national forecasting service still in operation anywhere. Supercomputers have long since replaced telegraphs, yet the goal remains the same. In 2022, the United Nations set the goal of covering every person on Earth with early warning systems before 2027 is over, since only half of all countries report having adequate ones.

Floating on its back and cracking open a clam on its chest, the sea otter looks more like a playful pet than a guardian of forests. Yet along the rocky coasts of the North Pacific, this small animal helps keep whole forests beneath the waves alive.

The animal's secret lies in its huge appetite. It has no thick layer of fat, as seals and whales do, to protect it from the cold. Instead, it depends on its fur, which is so dense that a single square inch can hold up to a million hairs, about ten times as many as on an entire human head. Staying warm this way burns a great deal of energy, so an adult must eat 20 to 30 percent of its body weight every day. Its menu includes clams, crabs, snails and sea urchins, and it often uses a rock as a tool to break open hard shells.

Those sea urchins are the key to the story. Urchins feed on kelp, a giant seaweed that can grow up to two feet a day and forms forests full of fish and other sea life. Where otters live in healthy numbers, they keep these spiny animals under control. Where otters disappear, the urchins multiply rapidly and can strip the forests bare. <b>This</b> is why scientists describe the sea otter as a keystone species, one whose presence holds an entire ecosystem together.

Because of their valuable fur, sea otters were hunted almost to extinction between 1741 and 1911. Before the hunting began, there may have been as many as 300,000 of them; by the early 1900s, only about 13 small groups survived. A treaty signed in 1911 finally protected them, and in the late 1960s, 413 otters were moved to Southeast Alaska, where their numbers recovered.

Today, about 3,000 southern sea otters live along the coast of California, but they have returned to only about 13 percent of their former range. Farther north, in waters the otters have not yet returned to, the kelp canopy along the 217 miles of coast between San Francisco and the Oregon border has shrunk by more than 90 percent after a marine heat wave and a population boom of purple sea urchins.

On December 17, 2020, UNESCO added sauna culture in Finland to its list of the world's intangible cultural heritage. The news hardly surprised the Finns themselves. Their country of about 5.5 million people has roughly three million saunas, and almost 90 percent of Finns take a sauna at least once a week. For them, sitting in the heat is less a special treat than a part of everyday life.

The habit has deep roots. The earliest Finnish saunas were smoke saunas dug into hillsides: stones were heated over a wood fire until smoke filled the room, and the smoke was let out before anyone went in. Later, when saunas became separate buildings with proper airflow, they took on many other tasks. Families heated and preserved food in them, and until the 1940s, many women in the countryside gave birth in them. When Finns moved to new land, they often built the sauna before the house.

Modern saunas are heated by wood or electricity, usually to around 80 degrees Celsius. When water is thrown onto the hot stones, a wave of steam called löyly rises. Some bathers gently beat their skin with a bundle of birch twigs, and in winter many cool off by jumping into a hole cut in a frozen lake or by rolling in the snow. People are usually naked, which Finns say puts everyone on the same level, and the room is kept quiet.

<b>This sense of equality</b> helps explain why the sauna is also a place for conversation. Friends relax there, colleagues sometimes discuss business, and during the Cold War, President Urho Kekkonen even held meetings with Soviet leaders in the sauna.

Scientists have also studied what the habit may do for the body. One Finnish study followed 2,315 middle-aged men for about two decades. Those who took a sauna four to seven times a week were about half as likely to die of heart and blood vessel disease as those who went only once a week. However, the study could show only a link, not proof that the heat itself was the cause, and its authors called for further research.

In 2019, scientists exploring the deep sea off central California steered a remotely operated vehicle across the floor of a marine sanctuary, more than 3,200 meters below the surface. Suddenly the skeleton of a whale appeared in its lights. People watching the live stream online saw fish, octopuses, and worms crowding over the bones. The team had found a whale fall: the body of a whale that has sunk to the bottom of the ocean.

To see why such finds excite researchers, imagine life in the deep sea. It is cold and completely dark, and food is scarce. Most of what animals eat there is “marine snow,” a slow drift of dead plankton and waste from the waters above. A dead whale changes everything. Researchers have estimated that a 40-ton whale can deliver to about 50 square meters of sea floor as much food as marine snow would bring in 2,000 years.

Since whale falls were first studied in 1988, scientists have learned that a single carcass feeds a series of different communities. First, sharks, hagfish, and other scavengers strip away the flesh within months. Next, small worms and crustaceans move into the mud around the skeleton, which is rich with scraps of tissue. The longest stage comes after that. Bacteria break down the fat stored inside the bones, producing a gas called hydrogen sulfide, and other bacteria use this gas for energy. Thick mats of bacteria, along with clams and other animals that depend on them, can live on a skeleton for years or even decades.

Some of the strangest residents are worms called Osedax, a Latin name meaning “bone devourer.” Researchers came across them in 2002 while they were searching for clams in a deep canyon off California, about 3,000 meters down. The females have no mouth or stomach. Instead, they send green roots into the bone and release acid to dissolve it, while bacteria living inside the roots digest the fats. Fossils show that these worms were already boring into whale bones 30 million years ago.

In the deep ocean, it seems, almost nothing goes to waste. Long after a whale dies, its body goes on feeding a world that few people will ever see.

In the early nineteenth century, carrying a living plant across an ocean was close to hopeless. Collectors packed young plants into boxes, loaded them onto ships, and hoped for the best, but few survived months at sea. In 1819 an East India Company surgeon in Macao estimated that only one plant in a thousand sent from China to London arrived alive.

The answer was found by accident. In 1829 Nathaniel Ward, a London doctor, buried the chrysalis of a moth in damp soil inside a glass bottle and closed the lid. The moth never came out, but a fern and some grass soon sprouted. Ward's own garden had done poorly, since the polluted air of east London poisoned his plants. Sealed off from that air, the seedlings in the bottle stayed healthy for years without being watered. Water given off by their leaves gathered on the glass and ran back down into the soil, where the roots could use it again.

Ward then tested the idea at sea. In 1833 he packed ferns and grasses into two glass-sided wooden cases and shipped them to Sydney, where almost all of the plants arrived alive. The cases were refilled with Australian plants for the trip home, a voyage of about eight months through temperatures from above 30°C to below freezing. Those plants, too, reached London in good health. In 1842 Ward described his method in a book, and his boxes became known as Wardian cases.

For roughly the next hundred years, nurseries and botanical gardens moved plants around the world in them; one nursery alone sent out more than 500. Tea, rubber, and bananas all crossed oceans this way. In many Victorian homes, glass cases full of ferns became fashionable, the ancestors of today's terrariums.

Yet the cases also held soil, and the soil was rarely empty. Insects, worms, and unwanted seeds traveled too, and many reached their new homes alive. In 1862 an American doctor returning from Japan brought Japanese honeysuckle home in Wardian cases; the vine later spread through woodlands across the eastern United States. In 1924 a senior US agriculture official warned that the cases had probably spread more harmful pests than almost any other carrier. Stricter rules followed. By the 1940s the cases had largely fallen out of use, and in the 1960s the last of them gave way to plastic bags and air freight, which let plants travel quickly and without soil.

When the Aztecs founded their capital, Tenochtitlan, in 1325, they chose an unlikely spot: an island in a lake, in the lowest part of a valley with no natural outlet for its water. To grow food, they built land of their own in the shallow water around them. These plots are called chinampas, and as the city grew, so did the chinampas that surrounded it.

They are often called “floating gardens,” but the name is misleading. A chinampa begins in a part of the lake only 30 to 60 centimeters deep, where the bottom is firm. Farmers mark out a long, narrow rectangle with stakes cut from a native willow, which take root and grow into trees. Reeds are woven between these stakes to form a fence, and the space inside is filled with water plants and then with mud scooped from the lake floor, layer after layer, until the surface rises above the water. As the plant matter rots, it turns into rich soil, while the roots of the willows hold the edges in place and fix the whole plot to the bottom of the lake.

The shape is no accident. A chinampa is usually no more than about 12 meters wide, so water from the canals on each side can soak through the soil to the middle. Farmers can also water their crops and gather the harvest from a canoe, and the canals, usually 4 to 6 meters wide, serve as roads for carrying goods to market. With good care, a single plot can produce several harvests a year.

After the Spanish destroyed Tenochtitlan in 1521, the old water system was largely neglected, and over the following centuries most of the lakes were drained to stop the city from flooding. Today, chinampa farming survives in only a few places, mainly around Lake Xochimilco in Mexico City, where farmers grow vegetables and flowers. Even there, the plots are shrinking as the city spreads. In recognition of their value, the United Nations’ Food and Agriculture Organization has named the chinampas a farming system of global importance.

A single extra zero can turn a debt of ten coins into a debt of a hundred. That simple fact helps explain why one of the most useful ideas in mathematics was once treated with suspicion.

Zero actually does two different jobs. As a placeholder, it keeps other digits in their proper positions, so that 2106 is not confused with 216. As a number, it can be added, subtracted, and multiplied like any other. The first job appeared long before the second. The Babylonians, who counted in sixties, used a place-value system for over 1,000 years with no mark at all for an empty place, leaving readers to work out the meaning from context. By around 400 BC, scribes were pressing two small wedges into their clay tablets to fill the gap, but only between digits, never at the end of a number.

Treating zero as a number in its own right came later, in India. In 628, the astronomer Brahmagupta set out rules for it: zero is what remains when a number is taken away from itself, adding zero changes nothing, and anything multiplied by zero becomes zero. Not every rule held up, however. He declared that zero divided by zero equals zero, which mathematicians today reject.

These ideas traveled west through Arab scholars and eventually reached a young Italian named Leonardo of Pisa, now known as Fibonacci. His father worked for Italian merchants in a North African port, and it was there that the boy learned the Indian numerals. In 1202 he completed Liber Abaci, a book that showed Europeans how to use them, filled with practical problems about prices, profits, and changing one currency into another. Even so, Fibonacci called 0 a “sign,” while he described the digits one to nine as numbers.

Merchants did not rush to adopt the new system. In 1299, the leaders of Florence, a city famous for its banks, banned the new numerals, including zero. A common explanation is that they feared fraud: with a stroke of the pen, 10 could become 100, a trick that was harder to play with Roman numerals. Yet calculating with Roman numerals was slow and clumsy, and over more than a century the new digits won out. By the fifteenth century they were widely accepted, and zero had finally earned its place.

Before 1922, a child found to have type 1 diabetes had little hope of growing up. The disease was often fatal, and the best treatment available, a diet promoted by Frederick Allen, offered patients only a few extra months. It strictly limited carbohydrates and made patients go without food again and again, so harshly that one leading doctor compared it to starving a child in order to keep him alive.

The turning point came in 1921 at the University of Toronto. Frederick Banting, a Canadian surgeon, persuaded the professor John Macleod to lend him laboratory space and a student assistant, Charles Best, who was reportedly chosen by the toss of a coin. That summer the pair collected a liquid extract from the pancreas. Macleod, doubtful at first, insisted on further tests, and in December the biochemist James Collip joined the team to make the extract purer.

On 11 January 1922, a 14-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson received the first injection at Toronto General Hospital. It failed: the dose was too small, and he had an allergic reaction. Collip spent the next 12 days improving the extract, and a second injection on 23 January brought Thompson’s blood sugar down sharply. He lived for 13 more years. Later that year, Elizabeth Hughes, the daughter of the American Secretary of State, arrived in Toronto. At 14, she was five feet tall but weighed only 45 pounds and could barely walk. About three months after starting insulin, she went home at more than twice that weight.

The four researchers sold the patent to the university for a token one dollar, partly so that no single company could control a drug that saved lives. The 1923 Nobel Prize went to Banting and Macleod, who split the money with Best and Collip.

Insulin is not a cure; most people with type 1 diabetes must still take it every day for the rest of their lives. Yet it has turned a deadly illness into a manageable one and has saved millions of lives. Banting’s wish for a drug that everyone could afford, however, has not always been honored: in the United States, a single vial of one modern insulin cost $21 in 1999 but $332 in 2019.
