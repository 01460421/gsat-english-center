AText A: Why We Should Bring Back the Mammoth

About 4,000 years ago, the last woolly mammoths died out. Today, we may finally have the tools to bring back something very much like them. The plan is not to clone a mammoth, which is impossible because frozen remains no longer contain living cells. Instead, scientists compare mammoth DNA with that of the Asian elephant, the mammoth's closest living relative, and look for the genes behind long, thick hair and the body fat that kept mammoths warm. Those changes can then be made in elephant cells, and the edited embryos could one day be carried by female elephants. The method has already been tried on a smaller animal: in 2025, researchers produced mice whose hair grew three times longer than usual.

Why go to <b>such trouble</b>? Mammoths once shaped the cold grasslands of the north. Herds of mammoth-like elephants could press down the snow and grass that cover the frozen ground like a blanket, slowing the thaw that releases carbon into the air. The same tools could also help protect animals that are disappearing right now.

BText B: An Ecologist Replies

I would love to see a mammoth one day, but I doubt that an edited elephant would really be one. A mammoth was not simply a hairy elephant. Genes work together in complicated ways, and changing a handful of them will produce, at best, a rough copy. The mice in that experiment certainly look woolly, yet no one has tested whether they can actually survive the cold.

There are practical problems, too. An Asian elephant carries her baby for about 22 months, and the species is itself endangered. Elephants also learn how to live from older members of their herd, so who would teach the first "mammoth" how to be one? Even its old home has changed greatly since the last ice age ended some 11,700 years ago.

Finally, there is the question of money. Species are vanishing every day, and every dollar spent on reviving a lost animal is a dollar not spent on saving the ones we still have.

第 39 至 42 題為題組

Children around the world sing “do, re, mi” long before they can read music. Few of them know that these simple sounds were taken from a Latin hymn about a thousand years ago, as part of one teacher’s effort to save singers time.

In the eleventh century, church singers learned their chants mainly by ear. The written signs of the day showed only whether a melody rose or fell, not exactly how far it moved, so every new tune had to be heard again and again until it stayed in memory. Mastering all the chants needed for the church year could take many years. Guido of Arezzo, a monk who trained the cathedral singers in the Italian city of Arezzo, saw <b>this</b> as a terrible waste of effort.

His answer had two parts. First, he placed notes on and between lines, so that a singer could see the exact pitch of each sound. Second, he gave the notes names. He chose a hymn to Saint John the Baptist whose first six lines each begin one step higher than the line before. The words of the hymn were already old, but the opening syllables of those lines, ut, re, mi, fa, sol and la, became labels for the six steps. A singer facing a new chant could now match each note to a tune he already knew by heart.

The method worked so well that Pope John XIX asked Guido three times to come to Rome and show it. The delighted pope then refused to let him leave until he himself had sung a melody he had never heard before.

The names have changed a little since then. In the 1600s, an Italian scholar suggested replacing ut with do, which is easier to sing because it ends in an open sound. A seventh name, si, was later added, probably from the first letters of Sancte Ioannes, the closing words of the hymn. In the 1800s, an English teacher proposed changing si so that no two names would begin with the same letter, which is why many people now sing ti. Still, the basic idea has lasted: students who sing a scale today are using Guido’s invention.

第 43 至 46 題為題組

When the Statue of Liberty was unveiled in New York Harbor in 1886, she looked nothing like the pale green figure on today’s postcards. Her skin, made of copper sheets about as thick as two pennies pressed together, had the warm brown shine of a brand-new coin. Within a few decades, that shine had disappeared.

The change was caused by the air itself. Copper reacts slowly with oxygen when it is left outdoors, and in the harbor the statue was also exposed to salt water and damp sea winds. Old black-and-white photographs suggest that she first grew darker, turning a deep brown or gray. Over the years, this dark surface was slowly replaced by a crust of blue-green copper compounds known as a patina. Since color photography did not yet exist, no one can say exactly when the change was complete. Experts at the National Park Service believe the whole process took 25 to 30 years, although the statue already looked sea green by 1906.

Many people assume the green coat is a sign of damage, much like rust on an old bicycle. In fact, it does the opposite. In many places the patina is as thick as the copper underneath, and it keeps the metal from slowly wearing away. Far from ruining the statue, the weather gave her a protective layer.

The real trouble lay where visitors could not see it. Inside the statue, iron bars connected the copper skin to the frame that holds it up, and over time they rusted badly. During a major restoration finished in time for the statue’s 100th birthday in 1986, workers replaced these bars with stainless steel ones. They also repaired holes in the copper, removed layers of old paint from the inside of the skin, and replaced the water-damaged torch with a gold-covered copy of the original design.

Today, chemistry teachers can produce a similar blue-green coating on copper coins in less than a minute. On the statue, <b>the same job</b> took wind, rain and sea air about three decades. Her famous color is therefore not a sign of neglect but a record of the many years she has spent standing in the open air.

For most of history, drinking water could make people seriously ill. Below are six inventions, old and new, that help people around the world get water that is safe to drink. A few depend on modern science, but many of them use materials that almost anyone can find.

ASand Filter

By the early 1800s, some towns in Europe were making river water safer by letting it sink slowly through a thick bed of sand. Dirt and many germs stay trapped between the grains. Villages still build sand filters today, because they can be made from local materials.

BChlorine

In 1908, Jersey City in the United States began adding chlorine to its drinking water to kill germs. Today, many families add a few drops of household bleach, which contains chlorine, to a bucket of water. Unlike most other methods, it keeps protecting the water while it is stored.

CClay Pot Filter

First made in Guatemala, this filter is a pot of clay mixed with sawdust. When the pot is baked in a very hot oven, the sawdust burns away and leaves tiny holes. Water drips slowly through the holes, while dirt and most germs are held back.

DSolar Bottles

This method needs only clear plastic bottles and sunshine. Fill the bottles, lay them on their side in direct sunlight, and wait at least six hours for the sun’s rays to kill the germs. If clouds cover the sky, wait two whole days instead. It does not work well with muddy water, though.

EFolded Cloth

In Bangladesh, scientists taught villagers to use an old sari, the long cloth that many women wear, as a filter for their drinking water. Folded four to eight times, the cloth caught tiny animals that carry cholera germs. In a three-year study, villages that used the cloth had about half as many cholera cases.

FStraw Filter

This pocket-sized tube lets a person drink straight from a river or a pond. As the user sucks water up, a layer inside the straw filters out bacteria, which get caught in holes too small for them to get through. Many travelers carry one, but it cannot reliably stop viruses, which are even smaller.
