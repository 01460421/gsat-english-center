Today, purple is everywhere, from school uniforms to sports shirts, and no one thinks of it as special. For most of history, however, wearing purple was a way of announcing that you were rich or powerful.

The most famous purple of the ancient world was Tyrian purple, named after the city of Tyre in Phoenicia, which produced much of it. Purple dyes of this kind were already being made 4,000 years ago. Their source was surprising: sea snails known as murex, which produce a mucus that knocks out their prey and keeps enemies away. Dyers left thousands of the snails to dry in the sun, boiled them, and removed the glands that make the mucus, a process that was slow and famously smelly. Because enormous numbers of snails were needed to color even a small piece of cloth, the dye could be worth more than gold.

That price turned purple into a badge of rank. From Phoenicia to Greece and Rome, the color came to stand for royalty, and in some places a person caught wearing it without the right social standing could be severely punished, even executed.

The color's fate changed in 1856, when William Henry Perkin, an 18-year-old student at London's Royal College of Chemistry, set out to make quinine, a medicine used against malaria. His experiment failed. Instead of the white crystals he had hoped for, he ended up with a dark, sticky mass. When he tested it further, however, part of it dissolved in alcohol into a brilliant purple liquid that could dye silk. Perkin patented the dye, which became known as mauve.

Mauve was cheap to produce and did not fade easily, and it quickly became fashionable. In 1859 the British humor magazine Punch joked that London had caught the <b>mauve measles</b>. Chemists soon created other synthetic dyes in bright shades of red, blue, and yellow, and by about 1860 such dyes were widely available. A color that had once marked kings could now be worn by almost anyone.

Each year in November or December, when it is time to sow millet, a group of Bunun men in Taiwan stand in a circle with their arms linked around one another. They sing no words, only vowels. One man starts on a single note; the others come in one by one beneath him, and the top voice keeps nudging the pitch upward in steps smaller than a half step, so the whole chord slowly climbs. The song, pasibutbut, is a prayer for a good millet harvest, and the Bunun believe that the better the singing, the happier their sky god and the richer the crop. When the last full chord fades, the men stop and lift their faces to the sky.

The wider world came to know the song through a recording. In 1943, the Japanese musicologist Kurosawa Takatomo recorded it in what is now Taitung County, with help from Japanese police and a young Bunun man. In 1952, he sent the recording to an international folk music council linked to UNESCO, and well-known Western scholars took notice. A story still often told in Taiwan holds that the song overturned a Western theory that music grew from single tones to two tones and only much later to harmony.

The song is also famous in Taiwan as the “eight-part harmony,” but the name is not exact, for the men sing in four parts. Between 1992 and 1995, the ethnomusicologist Wu Rung-shun studied 25 recordings with a machine that turns sound into pictures. He found that a throaty “e” sound and a special use of the throat produce strong overtones, higher tones that ring above the notes actually sung, so listeners hear many more lines than the singers produce. Wu notes that some villages have added singers in the hope of creating this effect, but extra voices alone cannot do it. In his view, the Bunun name is no myth: their ears had caught a real sound that outsiders missed.

Even among the Bunun, the song is not shared by everyone, since only two of the five Bunun groups traditionally sing it. Once bound up with ritual and taboos, it is now also performed on stage, and in 2015 Hualien County listed it as part of its cultural heritage.

第 43 至 46 題為題組

Every time someone streams a video or asks a chatbot a question, the request travels to a data center, a building packed with computers that run day and night. Those machines turn electricity into heat, and unless the heat is carried away, they cannot keep working properly. Carrying it away often takes water, and a surprising amount of it.

Many large data centers rely on cooling towers. Warm water from the cooling system is pumped into the towers, where part of it evaporates and takes the heat into the air. Water that leaves as vapor does not return to the river or lake it came from, so experts describe it as consumed rather than simply used. The remaining water can circulate several times, but each round of evaporation leaves minerals behind, and once they become too concentrated, the water must be drained away and replaced. Because more water evaporates in hot weather, these centers are thirstiest in the hottest hours of the day and the hottest months of the year. One study estimated that American data centers directly used about 17 billion gallons of water in 2023, roughly three times the 5.6 billion gallons used in 2014.

That figure leaves out a hidden share. Many of the power plants that supply data centers with electricity cool themselves with towers of their own, so every unit of power carries a water cost. The International Energy Agency estimates that only about 40 percent of the water linked to data centers is used on site; the other 60 percent is consumed at power plants.

Reducing this footprint is harder than it sounds. Air-cooled systems use no water at all, but they need more electricity than systems that depend on evaporation. Timing offers another option. Training runs for AI models, which can take weeks, are a different matter from everyday requests. Unlike web searches and video streams, which users expect to start instantly, <b>they</b> can be scheduled for almost any hour, including cooler ones when less water is lost. Yet the coolest hours are rarely the sunniest, so the time that saves the most water may not be the time when solar power is most plentiful.
